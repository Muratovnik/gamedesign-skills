# Constructing evidence, inference and answer

Use for puzzles, investigations and decisions about hidden states. The target
can be a deduction, a risky guess, several defensible answers
or an action under uncertainty. Choose that contract before constructing clues.

## Make the inferential relationship

Start with what the participant is asked to determine or do. Create the relevant
world facts and plausible alternatives, then give each alternative an observable
consequence. Choose where and how those observations can be encountered. If you
start from an evocative clue instead, work backward to a world that could produce
it and forward to a conclusion the participant could reasonably draw.

Keep four roles distinct in the representation:

| Role | Required content |
| --- | --- |
| World fact | What happened, which transformations are legal and which uncertainties the author leaves open |
| Available evidence | What the participant can inspect, hear, test or remember, at what time and with what reliability |
| Grounds and alternatives | Which observations distinguish which explanations; which external knowledge is assumed or taught |
| Expression and confirmation | How the participant submits an answer or acts, what it costs, what feedback reveals and what changes next |

Draw the necessary links or use cards. For an intended conclusion, identify
sufficient sets of grounds and their access conditions. A single reliable clue,
several complementary clues and independent routes are different constructions.
Three clues in one inaccessible room do not supply three independent routes.
State closed-world assumptions if elimination depends on a complete list of
candidates; the author silently knowing there are no other suspects is not a
usable premise.

Provide operations that produce new information: compare, test a tool, revisit
a location after learning a rule, ask a speaker or act on a hypothesis. A cycle
of reinterpretation can be useful. A circular gate that makes all initial
evidence inaccessible cannot support the intended start.

## Worked investigation: the third bell

Original synthetic paper puzzle. A public rule card lists the complete possible
causes of the opening at the third bell: Mira turned the wheel, Oren turned it,
or an automatic pulse opened it. Exactly one occurred; impersonation, hidden
operators and unlisted routes are excluded in this bounded puzzle. Participants
may travel between Pump, Terrace and Tower in
any order, inspect their cards for free and return to the council to answer.

| Evidence card | Encountered content | What it can establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P, Pump | “The drum records a manual opening at bell 3.” | A manual act rather than an automatic failure |
| T, Terrace | “Oren crossed outward at bell 2. The sole bridge was closed until bell 4; the complete gate record shows no return.” | Oren could not operate the bypass at 3, given the public route rule |
| V, Tower, original version | Vale directly witnessed Mira turn the wheel at bell 3 | Identity and act, if this witness channel is permitted and reliable |

The public roster plus P and T support “Mira manually opened it at bell 3.” V
provides another sufficient route in the original version. In an altered
version the tower shutter was closed: Vale heard the bell but could not see the
wheel. Change V to that limited account. P and T still support the conclusion;
retaining the old eyewitness line would violate
[actor knowledge](../../gameplay-design/references/entities.md#actor-knowledge).

The council accepts a named operator or automatic cause as a one-time committed answer, including
an early correct answer. The participant may optionally cite grounds, but no
read-all flag gates the result. The correct answer receives confirmation only
on commitment; an incorrect one becomes a disputed public accusation and ends
this version's investigation. Thus guessing is possible and carries a declared
consequence. This game cannot claim to certify reasoning from a correct name.
An alternative game could accept a structured argument or offer revisable
hypotheses; specify the new expression and feedback rules if that is the aim.

**Replay.** Visit Terrace then Pump, omit Tower, and name Mira. Both required
grounds are available and the answer is accepted. Alternatively, answer from
Vale's original testimony. With the altered shutter, that second route no longer
supports identity. The player can return to Pump and Terrace instead. Travel
order changes the route to knowledge, not historical truth.

## Revise what actually prevents the chosen work

If the participant never encounters a clue, change its access or presentation.
If it is encountered but cannot distinguish alternatives, change its content or
add a meaningful operation. If an appropriate answer is understood but cannot
be expressed, change the input vocabulary or representation. If an unchecked
confirmation channel gives unlimited cheap guesses, decide whether that is a
valid strategy or change its feedback and consequence.

**Counterexample and legitimate alternative.** Requiring all three visited-card
flags after the P+T route blocks a sufficient solution. Removing the gate fixes
that contract. An adventure that intentionally requires collecting three
physical seals has a different task and may keep the gate. Likewise, reliably
providing a core clue can preserve an interpretation task; in a searching game,
the search itself may be the valued activity. Neither policy is universal.

For help, hand the specific intellectual work to
[learning and help](learning-and-help.md#choose-what-help-does). For evidence
produced by world changes, use [world relations](../../game-world-narrative-design/references/world-relations.md).
For scene effects and testimony after commitment, use
[conditional story](../../game-world-narrative-design/references/conditional-story.md).
Joint generation of facts, witnesses and clues belongs with
[repertoire](../../game-content-design/references/repertoire.md), preserving these
dependencies. A language-dependent clue also needs the
[adaptation](../../game-design/references/adaptation.md) contract.

## Basis and transfer boundary

[Emily Short, Making of Counterfeit Monkey: Puzzles and Toys, 2013](https://emshort.blog/2013/01/24/making-of-counterfeit-monkey-puzzles-and-toys/)
describes relationships among a tool, source objects, desired results and puzzle
alternatives. [Laws and Hite, GUMSHOE SRD, CC version 2013, Gathering Clues and
Core Clues](https://pelgranepress.com/gumshoe/files/GUMSHOE%20SRD%20CC%20version.pdf)
provides a published alternative in which appropriate investigative action
reliably receives core information. These are a creator account and rules,
respectively, not comparative proof of a best puzzle form.

[Joris Dormans, Adventures in Level Design, 2010, §§2–3](https://pcgworkshop.com/archive/dormans2010adventures.pdf)
distinguishes mission and space representations in a generation method. The
evidence/grounds/answer construction above is a local synthesis, not an assertion
that any of these sources supplies a universal inference algorithm. The paper
puzzle can establish declared logical sufficiency and access; human recognition
of its relationships remains a separate observation.
