# Learning, external objectives and help

Use when a participant should acquire a usable capability or when assistance
changes the work of play. Read the external-objective branch
only when a learning aim outside the game is part of the brief. A prologue alone
does not require a teaching procedure.

## Construct learning and application

Define the distinction or operation the participant should be able to use:
recognizing a direction, testing a transformation, comparing quantities, choosing
a route or combining known moves. Recover prerequisites, prior explanations,
language, possible errors, the cost of trying and any assistance that is intended
to remain. Describe the later application before drafting instruction, so the
introductory activity prepares an actual decision.

Make a first situation in which the participant can encounter and try the
relation. Give informative feedback: show what changed and which interpretation
it supports, rather than merely flashing success. Then construct a new situation
that changes a feature relevant to use of the relation. Keep an agreed permanent
channel, such as spoken readout or a notebook. Remove only guidance that makes
the decision on the participant's behalf when independent decision is the goal.

**Worked game-learning episode.** In the third-bell investigation from
[inference](inference.md), the first interaction places a harmless practice drum
beside a handwheel. The participant may turn the wheel, inspect the drum, or ask
what the tool records. Turning adds a marked “manual” stroke. A motor pulse adds
a different “automatic” stroke. Both labels remain available in a legend.
Later, the investigation's actual drum shows a manual stroke without highlighting
an answer. The participant can inspect it and connect the observed act to other
evidence. This teaches an operation without disclosing the operator's identity.
Someone who already knows the notation can skip the practice and retain the
same access to the real drum.

The subsequent decision uses the notation in a different context, but this
authored sequence alone does not show that a person learned it. A supplied
record of independent application could inform that question; a transcript
generated by the designer cannot substitute for it.

## External learning objective

Connect the subject relationship to an actual participant decision and its
feedback. Specify whether the game should require the subject work, supply
material for discussion, or combine both. Use reliable subject material or
relevant expertise where the relation is not elementary or already established.
Then try a plausible strategy or misconception that could achieve the game
reward without the intended relationship. Revise the reward, task, feedback or
claim. A discovered shortcut refutes necessity in the stated model; it does
not establish what a real participant learned.

### Worked external objective: comparing proportions

Original synthetic tabletop exercise. The stated external objective is “compare
fractions when group sizes differ.” All lenses in each fictional lot have been
inspected, so the exercise asks about known lot proportions, not a statistical
estimate of a future population. Each round presents two cards with cracked and
total counts. The participant selects the lot with the smaller cracked fraction;
either is accepted on a tie. A count card and a spoken readout can remain visible
throughout. One decision is committed before feedback; no choice is irreversible
outside the exercise.

| Round | Left lot | Right lot | Consequence and feedback |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Supported introduction | 2 cracked of 10 | 3 cracked of 30 | Right is 1/10; left is 1/5. Show that repeating the left group three times gives 6/30 rather than 3/30. |
| New application | 4 cracked of 40 | 2 cracked of 10 | Left is 1/10; right is 1/5. Selecting the smaller raw count chooses the wrong lot. |
| Contrast | 5 cracked of 20 | 1 cracked of 20 | Right is 1/20; left is 1/4. With equal denominators, raw counts suffice. |
| Tie | 1 cracked of 10 | 2 cracked of 20 | Both are 1/10; accept either or “equal.” |

The original weak version highlights the right card in the introduction and
awards a “ratio expert” badge after that one click. In the altered version, the
new application has no selecting highlight and changes which side has the lower
fraction. It includes the misconception “fewer cracks always means the smaller
fraction” as a contrast. Reward the particular correct decision; do not award a
claim about expertise from one result.

If a discussion is part of the learning design, supply its usable prompts:
“Why did two cracks mean different things across rounds? When was comparing raw
counts sufficient? What does this exercise say about these inspected lots, and
what would be missing to predict tomorrow's production?” The last question
marks the boundary of the game data; it is not a lesson in causal inference.

The rule-to-feedback relation is inspectable and the smaller-count shortcut is
refuted by the new application. Guessing can still succeed on any finite set.
Actual learning needs evidence of appropriate independent application; transfer
outside the game needs a separate relevant task and its actual observations.
The design may instead prepare a discussion without making the concept necessary
for winning, provided that role is explicit.

## Choose what help does

Read the actual task and help content. Identify whether assistance notices,
remembers, explains an operation, connects facts, selects an answer or performs
an already chosen action. Then write the assistance at the strength and moment
needed. Its length or nominal “hint level” does not reveal how much intellectual
work it performs.

For the third-bell investigation, these are usable alternatives, not a mandatory
ladder:

| Help | Exact material or behavior | Work delegated; work remaining |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Persistent notebook | Reproduce only cards the participant encountered, labelled by source and event time; permit free personal notes | Delegates recall; comparison and commitment remain |
| Operational cue | “You can inspect the drum to distinguish a manual turn from an automatic pulse.” | Explains the available test; interpreting its result with other evidence remains |
| Relational cue | “Compare Oren's departure and the bridge closure with the time of the manual turn.” | Supplies the central comparison; much of the discovery of the relationship is gone |
| Full solution on request | “Mira turned it. The drum records a manual turn at 3, while Oren was beyond the only bridge until 4.” | Delegates deduction; choosing what to report may still matter |

Give the participant control of optional help when that is the intended access
policy; a requested permanent readout need not be withdrawn. Do not reveal an
unencountered clue, hidden role or another participant's private record through
an alternative channel unless that information change is deliberate. Joint and
physical access are owned by [access and return](../../game-participation-design/references/access-and-return.md),
and language changes by [adaptation](../../game-design/references/adaptation.md).

**Counterexample and alternative.** A “small nudge” that highlights the only
incriminating comparison can perform the whole deduction. Calling it subtle
does not preserve the original task. A full answer remains legitimate when the
participant chooses it or the design shifts to the responsibility of reporting.
Judge the remaining activity accurately; do not force every game to withhold
help or every learning episode to erase its permanent aids.

## Basis and transfer boundary

[Alex Beachum, Outer Wilds: A Game of Curiosity-Driven Space Exploration, 2013,
pp. 17–23 and 25–27](https://sbekumod.github.io/Assets/OWtesi.pdf)
describes a prototype introduction, its revision and remaining work. It supports
examining the relation between operational preparation and later use, not claims
about all properties of the 2019 release or comparative teaching effectiveness.

[Iacovides, Cox, McAndrew, Aczel and Scanlon, Game-Play Breakdowns and
Breakthroughs, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/07370024.2014.987347)
examines action, understanding and involvement through a small study with
observations, interviews and diaries. Its distinctions caution against treating
progress as understanding; they do not validate the exercises here.
[Short's Counterfeit Monkey account](https://emshort.blog/2013/01/24/making-of-counterfeit-monkey-puzzles-and-toys/)
contributes a repertoire of operations and connected puzzles. The learning
sequence, proportion game, help texts and their transfer criteria above are
original constructions; no human learning or preservation of felt independence
has been measured for them.
