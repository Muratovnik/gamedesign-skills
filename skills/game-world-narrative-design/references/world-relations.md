# Making world relationships encounterable

Use for a place, society, ecology, practice or material relationship that should
appear through play. A world may be authored, symbolic,
fragmentary, improvised or simulated, and may have no required plot.

## Build outward from a consequential relationship

Choose something that makes opportunities differ: access to water, a migration,
who repairs a shared tool, an obligation, a taboo, a route or incompatible
accounts of a past event. Identify the involved entities and the spatial,
resource, institutional or historical relation between them. Select the horizon
over which its consequences matter. A ten-minute vignette and an autonomous
seasonal ecology need different representations.

Make its encounterable manifestations. Place a material trace, a repeated
practice, a situated speaker or a permitted action that lets the participant
discover the relationship. Use more than an encyclopedia assertion when the
task calls for play. A place can reveal who maintains it through its tools and
paths, while an interaction reveals who may use it and whose work depends on it.

Choose which consequences the world itself must calculate and which you will
author. A small set of explicit state snapshots can express a relationship
without an economic or physical simulation. If participants can intervene
repeatedly and those interventions combine, define enough state and transition
rules to preserve the intended consequences. Autonomous stability is a further
claim requiring execution of that actual model.

## Worked fragment: the diverted harbour

Original synthetic world. The pump routes fresh water either to the dyers'
terrace or to the storm refuge, where water pressure holds a flood door closed.
A damaged tide gate prevents supplying both until repair. These are fictional
engineering rules of the fragment, not claims about actual hydraulic systems.

| Place and relation | Encountered material | Available action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pump, halfway between terrace and refuge | Two labelled pipes, a diverted wheel, a manual-stroke drum and fresh fibres around the emergency lever | Follow either pipe, inspect the drum, compare tool traces |
| Terrace, work depends on water supply | Empty dye bowls, washed-out sample cloth and workers moving cloth to a dry upper rack | Inspect the closed intake; help move a rack; ask what stopped |
| Refuge, shelter depends on the pressure | A taut door brace and names of households on sleeping mats | Inspect the brace; carry bedding; ask which work the diversion protected |
| Quay council, authority governs public repair | A board naming who can order a diversion and an unfinished petition sheet | Read the rule; report what happened; question how repairs are assigned |

This already supports a world walk with no investigation or required ending:
start at Pump, follow a pipe, inspect a consequence and perform one local act.
The four locations are connected by the two pipes and public footpaths. No
roll is needed to inspect their stated features. The participant may leave at
any point. Performing an act changes that trace: the rack is now raised, bedding
is delivered or a question is written on the petition. Revisit the place to see
the change. Those are authored state changes, not an autonomous social simulation.

The same fragment can support an investigation by connecting the drum to
[inference](../../game-information-design/references/inference.md). The answer
does not itself explain whether the diversion was justified. Different roles
can value the lost work and protected shelter differently. Use the canonical
[actor knowledge](../../gameplay-design/references/entities.md#actor-knowledge)
to decide what each speaker can claim and why it might withhold or dispute it.

## Change a relation and reconstruct its manifestations

Suppose the existing version says the terrace immediately stops all work when
the flow diverts. The new design adds a reserve tank holding two batches of dye
water. Make a real change in what can happen:

| Affected material | Original version | Altered version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Terrace action | No mixing while flow is diverted | Mix a batch while reserve is positive; each batch consumes one of the two units |
| Spatial trace | Dry intake and empty bowls | Dry intake, wet tank level marks, one hose to the bowls |
| Work after first batch | Still stopped | One stored batch remains |
| Work after second batch | Still stopped | Mixing stops until supply returns |
| Worker's line | “The bowls went dry when the wheel turned.” | “We can finish two batches from the tank. After that, we wait.” |
| Council's framing | Immediate loss of a working shift | Limited time to share the repair before stored water runs out |

The refuge benefit remains, while the cost has a different temporal shape.
Propagate that into an affected scene through
[conditional story](conditional-story.md), and into reusable resource dynamics
through [resources and development](../../game-systems-design/references/resources-and-development.md)
when the broader economy is actually part of the task. This fragment needs only
the two-unit reserve state to define its local action.

**Counterexample and alternative.** Adding the tank only to the lore while still
disabling both allowed batches leaves the promised possibility absent. Giving
unlimited mixing despite an explicit two-batch reserve breaks the opposite
relation. A symbolic procession in which bowls never deplete can be legitimate
if scarcity is expressed through another chosen relation; it need not become a
resource simulation to resemble this example.

## Compose perspectives without inventing cultural authority

Distinguish the declared world rule, a person's belief, a public institution's
version and an intentionally unresolved account. Put disagreement in the actual
material: whose name is recorded, whose damaged cloth is visible, who can speak
at the council and which history another location preserves. If the participant
can change a relation, show the consequence somewhere they can encounter.

For real cultures, institutions or practices, use relevant sources and
perspectives to determine the content. Formal consistency cannot establish
authenticity, and a simulated society can consistently encode a stereotype.
The harbour above is invented and supplies no evidence about any real community.

## Basis and transfer boundary

[McCoy and colleagues, Prom Week: Designing past the game/story dilemma, 2013](https://www.fdg2013.org/program/papers/paper13_mccoy_etal.pdf)
describes authored social relationships and the difficulty of making reasons for
actions legible. This supports the relation between state, visible action and
explanation; it does not supply universal psychology.

[The Rain World: The Watcher creator interview, Unity, 2025](https://unity.com/blog/exploring-procedural-design-rain-world)
describes relationships among procedural modules and cases of unwanted dynamics.
Its creator observations inform the choice of what should act autonomously;
they are not a comparative demonstration that simulation is always preferable.
The encounterable-manifestation procedure and the harbour are original local
transfers. Their formal relationships do not establish cultural reception or a
long-running world's stability.
